Problem 1 | Mentor's frameworkOutpatient specialty clinic

Problem 1: Incomplete requests create rework and can delay needed care.

1Problem statement

Specialists must translate changing payer rules and gather scattered records. Missing evidence can restart the review, delay scheduling, and keep patients waiting.

2Target user

A prior-authorization specialist in a medium-sized outpatient specialty clinic.

3Breaking point

Turn an unstructured payer policy into a complete, case-specific evidence packet.

4Evidence

43 requestsand 12 staff hours per physician each week; AMA 2024 survey, n=1,000. Self-reported.

5Success metric

First-pass completeness rate before substantive payer review.

4 / 12 | AMA 2024 survey: 94% of respondents reported care delays; 24% reported a serious adverse event tied to PA. Self-reported association evidence. Also see GAO-25-107342.
Problem 1 | Current journeyThe clinic controls preparation, not approval

Solution direction: Help staff prepare a complete request before submission.

1Receive clinician order
2Verify plan and authorization need
Break AFind the applicable payer requirements
Break BGather complete evidence across records
5Submit and answer requests
6Communicate and close status
How the proposed solution works

3-agent workflow (provisional)

1Policy Agent: finds the current approved payer policy and extracts cited requirements.
2Evidence Agent: matches authorized records to the checklist and flags missing or uncertain items.
3Packet Agent: prepares the review packet and tracks payer follow-up.

How it works: Each agent passes its output and sources forward; staff review before submission.

Human authority

The agent never decides medical necessity, invents clinical justification, alters records, or approves coverage.

5 / 12 | Cheap test: compare one service across three public payer policies and measure what rules handle versus what needs contextual interpretation.

Problem 2 | Mentor's frameworkMultispecialty medical group

Problem 2: Misrouted denials delay payment and leave patients uncertain about what they owe.

1Problem statement

Specialists must interpret unclear denial information across several systems. A wrong route delays correction or appeal, holds up provider payment, and can leave patients facing unresolved bills.

2Target user

A denial-management specialist in a multispecialty medical group.

3Breaking point

Classify the denial and route it to billing, coding, clinical review, appeal, or write-off review.

4Evidence

85 millionin-network claims denied in 2024; fewer than 1% appealed. KFF analysis of CMS data.

5Success metric

First-pass routing accuracy confirmed by an authorized reviewer.

7 / 12 | KFF analysis of CMS data: about 496 million claims reported; 19% of in-network claims denied; fewer than 1% appealed; 66% of appealed denials upheld. HealthCare.gov marketplace plans only.
Problem 2 | Current journeyClassification before appeal

Solution direction: Help staff route each denial correctly the first time.

1Receive denial and payer message
2Review claim, coverage, and history
Break AInterpret the real cause
Break BRoute to the correct authorized action
5Gather evidence and review
6Submit, monitor, and close
How the proposed solution works

3-agent workflow (provisional)

1Classification Agent: interprets the denial reason and reports confidence and uncertainty.
2Evidence Agent: gathers approved records, deadlines, and requirements for the proposed response.
3Routing Agent: recommends the authorized queue, assigns an owner, and monitors status.

How it works: The agents prepare and route the case; staff approve the action and any submission.

Human authority

The agent never invents documentation, changes clinical records, guarantees success, or independently makes medical or legal determinations.

8 / 12 | Cheap test: profile the public denial data and determine whether available reason categories are specific enough for useful routing.
Problem 3 | Mentor's frameworkHospital to home

Problem 3: An incomplete discharge handoff can leave critical follow-up undone after the patient gets home.

1Problem statement

Discharge planners coordinate appointments, instructions, and records across disconnected teams. If no one accepts the next step, patients may miss follow-up, lack clear medication instructions, or return for avoidable care.

2Target user

A discharge planner or nurse case manager sending adult medical patients home.

3Breaking point

Confirm that the receiving organization accepted responsibility for the next operational step.

4Evidence

60%had adequate medication follow-up requests in an AHRQ-cited multicenter review; evidence is older.

5Success metric

Closed-loop handoff completion rate within an approved follow-up window.

9 / 12 | AHRQ Re-Engineered Discharge follow-up tool: in the cited review, 14% of inadequately followed patients had a related revisit within 6 months. Older evidence; current scale still needs validation.
Problem 3 | Current journeyA human-centered but provisional option

Solution direction: Keep every required handoff owned until it is completed or escalated.

1Receive expected discharge plan
2Coordinate appointments and services
3Send approved instructions and summary
Break AConfirm receipt and ownership outside the hospital
Break BTrack open items after discharge
6Complete or escalate the handoff
How the proposed solution works

3-agent workflow (provisional)

1Plan Agent: turns the approved discharge plan into assigned operational tasks.
2Handoff Agent: sends authorized information and confirms receipt and ownership.
3Follow-up Agent: tracks open items and escalates missed or unaccepted handoffs.

How it works: The agents maintain the task trail; clinical and discharge decisions remain with staff.

Human authority

The agent never determines discharge readiness, reconciles medication clinically, interprets symptoms, changes instructions, or gives treatment advice.
